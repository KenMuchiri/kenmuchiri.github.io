Why I’ve Been So Silent

October 2, 2026 · Founder Journal · Kenya

It has been a quiet month online, but not an easy one in real life.

That is the honest answer to the question I have been getting a lot lately: why have I been so silent?

September 2026 has been a heavy month. Not because I have been idle. Quite the opposite. I have been busy in ways that do not always translate neatly into posts, updates, or polished social media content.

I have been building our edtech solution.

And that work has been demanding in a very practical sense.

Building our edtech solution has come with real technical challenges

Our edtech solution is not just another product idea we are trying to push out quickly. It is a real system with real requirements, real users, and real operational complexity.

There are technical decisions to make. Integration choices. Architecture questions. User flow decisions. Product assumptions. Data considerations. Security concerns. Compliance realities. Workflow design. Systems that need to feel simple on the front end while being robust behind the scenes.

And because it is a school product, the stakes feel different.

Schools need more than a working interface. They need consistency. They need systems that are dependable. They need processes that can handle the realities of school operations, where the pace is different from a startup office and the cost of failure is higher than a broken feature in an app.

So a lot of my time has gone into understanding the actual needs of the product, not just the excitement of the idea.

That is the part people rarely see from the outside.

Behind every visible launch plan is a mountain of technical thinking, debugging, iteration, and product decisions that do not make good social media content.

Then there is the slower reality of government processes

One of the biggest challenges has not been in the code; it has been in the system around the code.

Some of the work needed to move this forward depends on government services, approvals, or processes that are simply slower than the pace of a founder trying to build and launch.

That has had a real effect on the timeline.

When a process depends on institutions that move slowly, the work does not stop. It just pauses in ways that are frustrating and often invisible from the outside.

And when those delays happen, it compounds everything else. It affects planning. It affects partnerships. It affects launch timing. It affects how quickly we can move from prototype to real-world deployment.

There is a lesson in that, and it is not a comfortable one: building in environments where government systems are slow is not just about product strategy. It is also about operational patience, political navigation, and timing realities.

It has been humbling. It has also been a reminder that building something meaningful often means living with friction that is outside your control.

I have also been trying to bring in more clients for the other services

Cloud Shule is one stream of work. But I am also still trying to build the wider business.

That means chasing clients. Getting more people to understand what we do. Maintaining existing client relationships. Making sure the business can keep moving even as we build a longer-term product direction.

That has meant more sales conversations. More outreach. More proposals. More meetings. More back-and-forth. More explaining, more listening, more adjusting, more trying to find a clear match between what we offer and what people actually need.

It is not glamorous. It is not always exciting. But it is the reality of running a company.

Sales is a skill, and in these months it has been a constant exercise in discipline. Cold calls. Cold meetings. Conversations that do not always convert. Follow-ups that take time. Trying to turn interest into action.

That work takes energy. It takes time. And it does not always leave much room for anything else.

The CEO work has been just as demanding

There is another part of this that people often underestimate: the CEO role is not just strategy. It is administration. It is operations. It is coordination. It is making sure the business can actually run smoothly while people are trying to do real work.

That means making sure internal systems are in order. Making sure communication is clear. Making sure decisions are tracked. Making sure people know what is happening. Making sure tasks are not falling through the cracks. Making sure the business is not just moving, but moving in a way that can be sustained.

It is a strange kind of work. Much of it is not visible. It is not loud. It is not exciting. But it is necessary.

If the company is going to grow, then the administrative foundations have to be strong. Without that, product work, sales work, and technical work all become harder than they need to be.

And this is not just a one-time task. It is ongoing. It is a constant effort to make sure the company does not become messy just because the pace is high.

I have been sleeping less, working more, and trying to stay focused

This month has also been one of less social time and less sleep than I would like.

That is not something I say to complain. It is simply part of the reality of building while trying to hold multiple demands together.

There are days when the work is technical. There are days when the work is sales. There are days when the work is financial. There are days when the work is management. There are days when the work is just trying to keep the entire system from collapsing under its own weight.

And when all of that happens at once, it leaves less room for everything else.

It means fewer conversations, slower replies, less personal time, and a lot less quiet time to process the work.

That is the honest truth behind the silence.

This is the reality of building

I think this is one of the things founders rarely explain well.

People see the visible outputs — the launches, the news, the announcements, the product stories — but they do not always see the invisible labor behind them.

There are technical problems. Customer conversations. Slow institutions. Product decisions. Administrative work. Sales pressure. Leadership strain. Sleep loss. Strategic ambiguity. Uncertainty. It all sits underneath the work.

And yet, this is exactly what building a company looks like in the real world.

It is not a continuous stream of inspiration. It is a sequence of difficult, unglamorous decisions made under pressure.

That is why I have been quiet. Not because I have lost interest. Not because I have nothing to say. But because I have been doing the actual work required to make the next phase possible.

I am still here. I am still building. I am still learning. And I am still trying to do the work that will turn this into something durable.

That is the best explanation I have for September.
