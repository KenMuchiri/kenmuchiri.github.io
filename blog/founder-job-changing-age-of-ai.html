The Founder’s Job Is Changing: Building Companies in the Age of AI

September 2026 · AI & Business · Kenya

For a long time, building a technology company required access to things that were difficult to obtain. Good developers were expensive. Building software took months. Research took time. Design required specialised teams. Automation required significant technical investment. Even turning an idea into a working product could require considerable capital.

AI is changing that equation. A small team can now research faster, write software faster, create prototypes faster, analyse information faster, and communicate with customers at a scale that would have required a much larger organisation a few years ago.

That is exciting. But I think it creates a different problem for founders.

If everyone can build faster, what actually creates an advantage?

The cost of building is falling

We are entering an interesting period in technology. The barrier between an idea and a working prototype is becoming smaller.

A founder can describe a product, generate an initial interface, connect an API, create a database, analyse customer feedback, and iterate on the product with tools that were previously available only to specialised teams.

This doesn’t mean software engineers are becoming irrelevant. Quite the opposite. The role of a good engineer is becoming more valuable because the amount of technology a small team can build is increasing.

But the economics are changing. A team of three people can potentially accomplish what previously required ten. A founder can test five ideas before committing significant resources to one. A business can automate processes that previously required several people to perform manually.

Kenya is already moving in this direction. Businesses are being pushed beyond simply experimenting with AI and toward demonstrating measurable returns from it. That distinction matters, because using AI is not the same as becoming an AI-powered business.

AI does not fix a bad business

This is probably one of the most important things I’ve learned while building. Technology can make a good process faster. It can also make a bad process faster.

You can automate a workflow that nobody needs. You can build an impressive product that nobody wants. You can deploy an AI chatbot that creates more frustration than it solves. You can generate thousands of pieces of content and still have nothing meaningful to say.

The availability of technology doesn’t remove the responsibility to think. It makes thinking more important.

The question is no longer simply, “Can we build this?” It is: “Should we build this? Who is it for? What changes if we succeed? How will we know that it is working?”

Those are business questions. They are also leadership questions.

The founder becomes the bottleneck

There is an uncomfortable side to all of this. When technology becomes easier to access, founders can become the biggest constraint in their own companies, not because they aren’t working hard, but because they are working on too many things.

There is always another feature, another tool, another AI model, another meeting, another idea, another potential customer, and another opportunity that looks too good to ignore.

AI can make this worse because it removes some of the friction that previously forced us to prioritise. When building something was expensive, you had to choose. When building becomes cheap, you can build everything. And building everything is another way of building nothing particularly well.

This is why I increasingly think one of the most valuable founder skills in the AI era will be judgment: knowing what deserves attention, what to ignore, when to build, when not to build, and when a problem needs technology rather than a better process.

Execution still matters

AI can make building easier. It cannot make execution automatic.

A prototype is not a company. A landing page is not a business. A chatbot is not a strategy. A product demo is not product-market fit.

Someone still has to speak to customers, understand the market, sell, negotiate, build trust, and make difficult decisions when the information is incomplete. Someone has to take responsibility when something goes wrong.

This is particularly important in Africa. We have enormous opportunities to apply AI to education, healthcare, agriculture, financial services, logistics, commerce, and public services. But building for these markets requires more than importing the latest technology.

Connectivity matters. Cost matters. Language matters. Trust matters. Payment behaviour matters. Culture matters. Sometimes the best technology is not the most sophisticated technology. It is the one people can actually use.

The new competitive advantage

So what happens when everyone has access to increasingly powerful AI tools? I don’t think the answer is simply “use AI better.”

The advantage will increasingly come from the combination of good judgment, speed, domain knowledge, distribution, trust, execution, and taste.

Good judgment means knowing which problems are worth solving. Speed means moving from idea to experiment without spending months preparing. Domain knowledge means understanding the industry and customer deeply enough to recognise problems that outsiders miss. Distribution means being able to reach the people who need what you are building. Trust means creating products and companies that people are comfortable relying on.

And perhaps most importantly, taste means knowing what good looks like. AI can give you ten versions. A founder still has to know which one is worth keeping.

What this means for AlphaAI

This is increasingly how I think about building AlphaAI. I don’t want AlphaAI to simply be a company that uses AI because AI is fashionable. There are already enough companies doing that.

The opportunity is to use technology to solve real problems, particularly problems that businesses and organisations in our environment experience every day. Sometimes that means building software. Sometimes it means automation. Sometimes it means an AI agent. Sometimes it means improving an existing workflow rather than creating something new.

And sometimes the right answer is to tell a client: You don’t need AI for this.

I think being able to say that is just as important as knowing how to build the AI. Technology should serve the problem, not the other way around.

The next generation of companies will be different

I think we are going to see very small teams build surprisingly large companies, not because people will stop being important, but because technology will allow good people to operate with extraordinary leverage.

A founder with a strong understanding of their market, a capable technical team, and the right AI tools can move incredibly quickly. That changes what it means to be a young company. It also raises the standard.

If everyone can move faster, speed alone stops being a competitive advantage. If everyone can generate content, content alone stops being a competitive advantage. If everyone can build software, software alone stops being a competitive advantage.

The question becomes: What are you building that is actually worth building?

That is the question I find myself coming back to. The future of entrepreneurship isn’t going to belong to the people who simply have access to the best AI tools. It will belong to the people who know what to do with them.

AI is making building easier. So the quality of our decisions matters more than ever.
