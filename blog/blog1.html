AI & Africa

Why Africa Needs to Build AI, Not Just Buy It

September 2026

Every AI headline this year has been about the same handful of labs, in the same handful of countries. Africa shows up in these stories almost exclusively as a market — a place AI gets sold into, once it’s already been built somewhere else.

That framing has a cost. Models trained on foreign data don’t generalize well to African languages, African transaction patterns, or African business behavior. Tools priced for economies with far higher willingness to pay stay out of reach for the businesses that could actually use them. And the assumptions baked into most AI products — reliable connectivity, formal banking, English as a default — simply don’t hold for most of the continent.

This is not an argument that every African company needs to train a foundation model from scratch. That would confuse ownership with imitation. Building means deciding which problems deserve attention, collecting and protecting the right data, adapting systems to local conditions, and having enough technical understanding to question the defaults we inherit. Sometimes the right answer will be an open model. Sometimes it will be a partnership. Sometimes it will be a small, carefully designed system that does one job extremely well.

The important thing is that the decision should be ours. When a tool is built elsewhere, the people who made it also made assumptions about language, workflow, risk, pricing, and success. Those assumptions may be reasonable for their users and still be wrong for ours. If we only buy finished products, we inherit those decisions without gaining the capability to change them.

Data is part of that capability, but data should not be treated as raw material waiting to be extracted. A farmer’s records, a patient’s history, a student’s progress, or a small business’s transaction trail represents a relationship of trust. Building African AI means creating clear rules about consent, privacy, access, and value. The goal is not simply to produce larger datasets. It is to make systems that understand context without turning the people who create that context into a resource to be mined.

Language makes the same point. An assistant that technically supports English but misunderstands the ways people actually mix languages, express urgency, or describe a problem is not truly accessible. African users should not have to translate their lives into the preferred vocabulary of a distant product before the product can help them. Local language support is not a cosmetic feature; it changes who gets to participate.

Infrastructure matters too. A useful system has to survive intermittent connectivity, uneven device access, mobile-first workflows, and budgets that cannot absorb expensive usage forever. These constraints are not annoyances to be designed around after the fact. They are design information. They tell us what reliability means, where intelligence should live, and how a product earns the right to remain in someone’s daily work.

There is also a talent question. Africa does not lack people capable of building ambitious technology. It lacks enough pathways for those people to work on consequential problems with the support, capital, and patience that serious products require. Every local product that reaches users creates more than a feature. It creates experience, technical judgment, institutional memory, and a reason for the next builder to believe the work is possible.

Of course, building locally does not mean building in isolation. The strongest future will be collaborative: researchers sharing methods, companies sharing lessons, communities shaping what gets built, and African teams contributing to global technical standards. Independence is not separation. It is having enough agency to enter the relationship as a contributor rather than only as a customer.

None of this is a reason to wait for someone else to fix it. It’s the reason I started AlphaAI. If Africa is only ever a consumer of AI built elsewhere, we pay rent on intelligence instead of owning any of it. I don’t think that has to be permanent — I think it’s a starting condition, and starting conditions can change.

The work will be uneven. Some experiments will fail, some products will be too early, and some ideas will turn out to be better suited to a different market or a different moment. That is normal. A continent cannot build durable technical capability by waiting until every attempt is guaranteed to succeed. It has to make room for iteration, honest evaluation, and the quiet accumulation of things learned.

That’s the whole thesis, really. Build AI on African data, for African problems, to a genuinely world-class standard. Everything else is execution.
