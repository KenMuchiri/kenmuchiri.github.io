Designing for Constraints

September 2026

The easiest way to design an AI product is to imagine perfect conditions. The user has a fast connection, a new phone, plenty of time, clear language, and complete trust in the company behind the screen.

Those conditions are useful for a demo because they remove friction. They are dangerous as a product strategy because the friction is the product. Connectivity, language, cost, trust, and time are not obstacles waiting for engineering to solve later. They are the environment the system has to understand from the start.

In Nairobi, a user may move between reliable Wi-Fi, an expensive data bundle, and a connection that disappears as soon as they leave one building. A helpful product cannot treat those situations as edge cases. It should preserve progress, keep the important work lightweight, and make failure recoverable.

Language is another form of context. Supporting English in a settings menu is not the same as understanding how people actually speak, mix languages, ask for help, or describe urgency. The system does not need to imitate every expression perfectly. It does need to respect the language people use to make decisions in real life.

Affordability changes the design too. When every interaction has a cost, the product has to earn the next interaction. That means reducing unnecessary requests, making value visible quickly, and resisting features that exist only because they look impressive in a presentation. Efficiency is not merely a technical virtue. It is a way of respecting the user's budget.

Trust is even more important when a system touches money, health, education, or work. People need to know what the system used, what it is uncertain about, and how to correct it. A confident answer is not a trustworthy answer if nobody can tell where it came from or what happens when it is wrong.

Designing for constraints does not mean making a lesser version of a product. It often makes the product clearer. When a team has to decide what must work offline, what deserves a human review, and which action matters most, the product becomes more focused for everyone.

This is the opportunity in building for African realities. The constraints force better questions: what is the smallest useful workflow, where should intelligence live, and what kind of help actually changes an outcome? Those questions can produce products that are more durable than systems built around abundance.

Good technology does not ask people to become ideal users before it becomes useful. It starts with the world as it is, then builds enough capability to help people move through it with more agency.
