The Challenges of Pre-Seed Startups

October 2026 · Founder Journal · Kenya

The pre-seed stage is a strange place to be in a startup.

On one hand, it feels like the beginning of something exciting. There is momentum. There is possibility. There are ideas that seem big enough to matter. There is a sense that the future might be visible if you just keep moving.

On the other hand, it is full of uncertainty. You are trying to build something real while operating with limited capital, limited traction, and a constant feeling that every decision carries more weight than it should.

That is the reality of a pre-seed startup. It is not glamorous. It is often exhausting. And it is usually much harder than the startup stories we see online make it look.

The first challenge is uncertainty

At pre-seed, you do not have the luxury of a clear market signal telling you exactly what to build. You are still searching for product-market fit. You are still learning what people actually need, what they will pay for, and what problem is valuable enough to justify real attention.

That means every decision feels like a bet.

Do we keep building this feature? Do we pivot this direction? Do we sell to businesses first or focus on individual users? Do we spend more time on product or customer research? Do we build an MVP now or wait a little longer?

Very few of these decisions feel certain. That uncertainty is normal. But it is also emotionally draining. It means you are constantly balancing optimism with pragmatism.

In other words, you are trying to build a business in a fog.

The second challenge is product pressure

There is always pressure to build fast. The market is moving faster. Competitors are emerging. AI tooling makes it easier to prototype. And the temptation is to chase speed at the expense of clarity.

But speed without focus becomes noise.

Pre-seed founders often feel like they must build everything at once because the market is competitive. They start adding features because they feel they are “missing something.” They keep extending the product because the idea is promising, but they have not yet validated the core value.

That is where many startups get stuck.

The real challenge is not just building. It is deciding what not to build.

Every feature is a claim about what the customer values. Every additional layer of complexity is a cost. And at pre-seed, you need clarity more than complexity.

That is a lesson I am learning in a very real way: the strongest product decisions are often the ones that say no.

The third challenge is traction

Traction is hard to come by at pre-seed.

Most startups do not have a large customer base. Most do not have instant proof that the market is ready. Most are still trying to understand whether people will return, recommend, or pay.

That can be frustrating because it creates a kind of invisible pressure. Everyone around you is discussing growth, scale, and momentum, while you are still figuring out whether the first version works at all.

It is easy to feel behind when you do not have a visible momentum curve yet. But the truth is that early-stage traction is not always linear. Sometimes it looks messy. Sometimes it appears in small signals before a big shift happens.

What matters is learning, not looking impressive.

The challenge is staying disciplined enough to gather real signal instead of chasing the illusion of progress.

The fourth challenge is money

Pre-seed startups are usually working under financial constraints. That does not always mean there is no money. It means there is not enough money to make mistakes carelessly.

Cash shapes decisions. It limits hiring. It affects how fast you can test. It affects how much risk you can take. It pushes you toward lean systems and sharper thinking.

That is not always a bad thing. Financial constraints can force clarity. They can force a founder to focus on the profitable and useful path rather than the shiny and broad one.

But they also create stress. They create pressure to make revenue earlier than is comfortable. They make it harder to invest in the deeper work that might matter in the long run. And if you are not careful, they can push you into building something for survival instead of building something truly valuable.

That tension is real. And it is one of the hardest parts of being a founder at this stage.

The fifth challenge is the founder’s emotional load

There is an emotional burden to pre-seed work that is not spoken about enough.

You are constantly juggling product, sales, customer conversations, team dynamics, research, admin, and your own uncertainty. Some days feel productive. Some days feel like nothing is moving. Some days it seems like every problem is bigger than you are ready for.

And yet, the startup must keep moving.

That means founders often do not get to feel too much uncertainty at once. They have to keep moving anyway. They have to make decisions before they feel fully ready. They have to deal with ambiguity without losing their belief in the work.

That is difficult, because early-stage startups do not reward certainty. They reward steadiness.

Steadiness is not excitement. It is discipline. It is doing the next necessary thing without getting thrown off by every setback. It is learning to tolerate slow feedback and not confuse delay with failure.

How I am navigating it

I am learning that the way through pre-seed is not by pretending the pressure is not there. It is by learning how to hold it without losing direction.

I am trying to be honest about what is uncertain. I am not pretending to know the answer before I have evidence. I am listening to the market. I am learning from customer conversations. I am paying attention to where the real friction is. I am trying to build from actual problem-solving, not from bright ideas alone.

I am also learning that product work and business work have to be treated as one continuous conversation. A startup is not just a product. It is a system of trust, distribution, understanding, and repetition. If the product is good but the business model is weak, the startup still struggles. If the business model is promising but the product is not useful, the startup still struggles. Both matter.

So I am working on all of it at once: product direction, customer conversations, business realities, and operational discipline. I am trying to build a company that can survive the early stage without getting lost in the mythology of startup speed.

That means being realistic. It means accepting that some parts of the work are slow and invisible. It means realizing that a founder’s job is not only to build; it is to create enough clarity to keep the company moving in the right direction.

And it means staying patient while the proof is still forming.

The real lesson

I think the real challenge of pre-seed startups is not that they are impossible. It is that they force you to become more honest, more focused, and more resilient than you were before.

It reminds you that building a startup is not a sprint with a clear finish line. It is a long sequence of small tests, uncomfortable decisions, and strategic adaptations.

It is humbling.

And it is also clarifying.

Because at the pre-seed stage, there is no room for pretending. You either learn, adapt, and move with purpose, or you get dragged by the weight of ambiguity.

That is how I am navigating it right now: with a bit more patience, a bit more focus, and a lot more respect for the work required to turn an idea into something real.
