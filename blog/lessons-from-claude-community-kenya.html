What I Learned from the Claude Community in Kenya

October 2026 · Founder Journal · Kenya

Some of the most useful things I have learned about building technology have not come from a course, a product announcement, or a late night with a new tool. They have come from conversations with other people trying to build.

Being part of the Claude community in Kenya gave me the chance to meet young Kenyans who are exploring what is possible with AI and, more importantly, working out what they can make useful with it.

I went in interested in the technology. I came away thinking just as much about the people: their ideas, their questions, the problems they notice, and the different paths they are taking to turn an idea into something real.

The builders are already here

It is easy to talk about African technology as if it belongs to the future: the talent we might develop, the companies we might create, the problems we might eventually solve. Meeting young builders makes that language feel outdated.

People are already experimenting, learning, and creating. They are using the tools available to them to explore ideas, test workflows, build prototypes, and imagine products that answer needs they understand first-hand.

Not every project is a company yet. Not every experiment will become a product. That is beside the point. Building is a process of asking better questions, testing assumptions, and learning where an idea meets reality. The energy in the room came from people willing to begin that process.

That changed my perspective. The conversation should not only be about whether Kenya has the potential to participate in the AI economy. We should also pay attention to the capability already taking shape and ask what would help it grow.

Tools matter, but context matters more

It is exciting to see what a capable AI tool can help someone do. It can make it easier to explore an unfamiliar subject, draft an early version, write code, or move from a rough idea to something people can react to.

But access to a tool is only the beginning. The more interesting question is what someone chooses to do with it. What problem do they understand well enough to notice? Who are they building for? What would make the result trustworthy and useful in that person’s real circumstances?

Those questions cannot be answered by a model alone. They come from paying attention to people, industries, languages, constraints, and daily routines. The strongest ideas are often connected to knowledge that does not appear in a prompt: lived experience, relationships, local context, and the patience to listen.

That is one reason local builders matter. Proximity to a problem can reveal details that an outsider misses. It does not guarantee a good solution, but it gives a builder a valuable place to start.

The value of being in the room

There is a particular kind of learning that happens when you meet people working on different ideas. You hear how they describe their challenges. You see what they are curious about. A question from one person can make another person reconsider an assumption they had been carrying.

Those conversations create connections, but connection is more than collecting contacts. It is the beginning of shared context. You learn who is exploring similar questions, where your experiences overlap, and where someone else sees a possibility you had not considered.

That kind of community can make the work feel less solitary. Building often involves long stretches of uncertainty, and it is easy to assume that everyone else has a clearer plan. Meeting other builders reminds you that many people are figuring things out in real time, too. The difference is that they keep asking, testing, and learning.

I left with new connections and a stronger sense that conversations like these should continue beyond a single gathering. The value compounds when people keep sharing what they learn, help one another through practical obstacles, and create room for new builders to join in.

A shift in perspective

Before, I could think about an AI community mostly in terms of the technology being discussed. After spending time with the Claude community in Kenya, I found myself thinking more about the conditions that help people build: access to tools, yes, but also confidence, useful feedback, peer support, and opportunities to put ideas in front of real users.

It also sharpened my sense of responsibility. It is not enough to be impressed by what AI can generate. As founders and builders, we have to decide what deserves to be built, what should remain in human hands, and how to make sure the people affected by a product have a meaningful place in its design.

That is true whether someone is working on a small experiment or a product intended to serve a large market. Ambition matters, but so does care: care for the problem, the user, and the consequences of getting it wrong.

Leaving inspired, returning to the work

I left encouraged by what I heard and by the people I met. Not with the idea that everything will be easy, or that access to AI tools removes the hard parts of building. It does not. Good products still require judgment, execution, trust, and a willingness to learn from what does not work.

But I left with a clearer picture of the people doing that work here. Young Kenyans are not waiting for permission to imagine what comes next. They are exploring, making connections, and finding ways to turn possibility into practice.

That is inspiring because it makes the future feel less abstract. It is not something arriving from somewhere else. It is being shaped, imperfectly and collaboratively, by people choosing to build now.

I am grateful to have been part of the community and to have met builders whose work and questions stayed with me. The conversations changed what I was paying attention to. They reminded me that building is not only about what one person can make; it is also about what becomes possible when people learn alongside one another.

There is a lot to learn from the community forming around AI in Kenya. I am looking forward to staying connected, continuing the conversations, and returning to my own work with that wider perspective in mind.
